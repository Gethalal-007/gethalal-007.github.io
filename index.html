🌸 Corolla-Generative-Art - Grow Colorful Petal Art Instantly

Turn a short line of text into a layered flower of petals, rings and dashes. Corolla is a seed-based generative art system that builds smooth, spiky and dashed petal rings around a central radius, tints them from a full colour spectrum, and places them on a deep dark ground. Every seed you type produces the same picture every time, so you can collect, share and recreate thousands of textile-style compositions.

✨ What Is Corolla?

Corolla is a small creative studio that lives in your web browser. Instead of drawing petals by hand, you give it a seed — a word, a phrase or a string of characters — and the program decides how many petal rings to grow, how wide each one is, whether the petals are rounded, pointed or broken into dashes, and which colours from the rainbow spectrum to use.

Because the seed controls everything, the results are reproducible. Type linen on your computer and type linen on a friend's computer and you will both see exactly the same flower, with the same colours, the same ring order and the same petal count. That makes Corolla a catalogue-building tool as much as a drawing tool: you can write a seed down on a sticky note, return weeks later, and bring the identical artwork back to life.

The project is built with standard web technology — HTML, CSS and JavaScript, with the drawing powered by the p5.js creative-coding library — so there is nothing heavy to install and nothing to configure.

🎨 What You Can Make

Corolla sits at the meeting point of generative art and textile design. The compositions it produces look like printed fabric, embroidered medallions, woven circular motifs or screen-printed scarf patterns. People use it for:

  • Fabric and surface patterns — repeating petal medallions that can be traced, recoloured or printed as swatches.
  • Poster and print art — bold dark-ground florals with vivid spectral rings.
  • Mood boards and colour studies — exploring how a single palette spreads across stacked rings.
  • Design teaching — demonstrating how a small set of rules can create enormous visual variety.
  • Personal catalogues — keeping a notebook of seeds that produced flowers you loved.

💻 Before You Begin

Corolla runs on a normal Windows computer with an up-to-date web browser. To get the best experience, make sure your machine matches the following:

  • Operating system: Windows 10 or Windows 11 (64-bit).
  • Browser: Microsoft Edge, Google Chrome or Mozilla Firefox, updated to a recent version.
  • Memory: 4 GB of RAM is plenty for smooth drawing.
  • Screen: any modern display; a bigger window simply means a bigger flower.
  • Internet: needed only to download the files. After that, everything runs offline.
  • Extras: no account, no licence key and no payment details are required.

🖱️ Running Corolla on Windows

Follow these steps in order. The whole process usually takes under two minutes.

  1. Open the download link above in your browser and save the Corolla files to your computer. By default, Windows puts downloads in your Downloads folder.
  2. Open File Explorer from the taskbar and navigate to your Downloads folder.
  3. If the folder you received is compressed, right-click it and choose Extract All…, then confirm. Extracting keeps all the files together so the artwork can load correctly.
  4. Open the extracted Corolla folder. Look for the main page file, which is named index.html.
  5. Double-click index.html. Your default browser opens and the Corolla canvas appears against its dark background.
  6. If the page opens in an unexpected program, right-click index.html, choose Open with, and pick Microsoft Edge, Google Chrome or Mozilla Firefox.
  7. Type a seed word into the input area and press Enter or click the generate control. A flower of layered petals appears on screen.
  8. Keep the browser tab open while you explore. Change the seed whenever you want a new composition.

🌱 How a Seed Becomes a Picture

Every letter and number in your seed is turned into a number, and those numbers drive a chain of decisions. First Corolla chooses how many petal rings the flower will have. Then it decides the radius of each ring, so some flowers are tight and dense while others are open and airy. Next it picks a style for each ring — smooth, spiky or dashed — and finally it walks around the colour spectrum to decide the tint of every petal and every stroke.

Nothing is random in the moment. The seed is the whole story. Change one character and the entire flower reorganises; keep the seed identical and the flower never changes. This is why Corolla works so well as a catalogue: seeds are the catalogue numbers.

🧵 Three Petal Styles

🌼 Smooth petals

Soft, rounded lobes that read like embroidered satin stitch. They give a calm, organic base to a composition.

⭐ Spiky petals

Sharp, pointed lobes that look like cut paper or folded fabric. They add energy and a crisp rhythm.

➰ Dashed rings

Broken outlines and stitched segments, reminiscent of running stitch and printed ticking. They let the dark ground breathe through.

Most flowers combine all three. A typical composition begins with a smooth outer ring, tightens into spiky inner petals, and finishes with a dashed centre ring that pulls the eye inward.

🌈 Colour from the Spectrum

Corolla tints its petals by sampling the spectrum, so each ring tends to sit slightly further around the colour wheel than the ring before it. The result is a natural gradient from warm to cool — coral into amber, amber into teal, teal into violet. Because the dark ground stays deep and quiet, the petals read as bright textile on a night-coloured cloth.

The spectrum sampling is also controlled by the seed, so the palette is part of the reproducible recipe. Two seeds that produce identical petal counts may still look completely different because their colour walk starts in a different place.

🖼️ Saving and Sharing Your Compositions

  • Save the seed: write the seed word down exactly as typed, including capitals and spaces. The seed is the artwork.
  • Save an image: use your browser's screenshot or print-to-file option to capture the canvas as a picture.
  • Share a recipe: send someone the seed text and they can rebuild the identical flower on their own machine.
  • Build a catalogue: keep a simple text file listing seeds you like, one per line, in the order you discovered them.

🛠️ Changing the Settings

If you are comfortable opening a text file in Notepad, the Corolla folder is simple to explore. The visual controls — ring counts, petal density, stroke weight and colour range — are plain values that you can adjust and save. Change a number, save the file, then refresh the browser tab with F5 to see the effect.

Keep a copy of the original file before you edit it. Because the seed system decides so much of the outcome, small changes to the settings produce noticeably different flowers across every seed you try.

📁 Inside the Corolla Folder

  • index.html — the page you open to launch Corolla.
  • style.css — the colours, spacing and dark ground of the interface.
  • sketch.js — the drawing rules for petals, rings, dashes and spectrum tints.
  • A p5.js library file — the drawing engine that paints the canvas.

Keep these files in the same folder. Moving one away from the others will stop the artwork from appearing.

❓ If Something Does Not Look Right

  • Blank dark page: confirm you opened index.html and not another file, and that all files sit together in one folder.
  • Nothing happens when you type: refresh the tab with F5. If that does not help, try a different browser such as Edge or Chrome.
  • Odd blocky edges: enlarge the browser window or lower your display zoom with Ctrl and − to give the canvas more room.
  • Same flower every time: the seed is unchanged. Edit the text, even by one character, to generate a new composition.
  • File will not open: right-click the file, choose Properties, and make sure it is not blocked, then try Open with and select your browser.

🧶 Using Corolla for Textile Design

Designers working with fabric, print or embroidery often treat Corolla as a sketchbook. Generate twenty flowers from twenty seeds, print the ones that feel balanced, and use them as references for weave structure, dye placement or repeat patterns. The smooth rings suggest woven curves, the dashed rings suggest stitch lines, and the spectral tints suggest dye gradients.

Because each seed is permanent, a finished textile run can be documented completely: note the seed, note the settings, and the exact source artwork is always recoverable. Over time that record becomes a personal archive of computational textile compositions — a catalogue you built one character at a time.
